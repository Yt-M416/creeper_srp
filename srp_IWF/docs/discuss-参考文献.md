# Executive Summary  
This annotated discussion evaluates how circadian disruption from transmeridian travel (jet lag) impairs athletic performance. We link our findings to recent studies showing that travel-related sleep loss and circadian misalignment reduce next-day physical output and game metrics. The mapping table identifies key citations by paragraph, and the reference list includes the original and newly added sources with notes on their relevance and usage.

## Annotated Discussion Paragraph

Jet lag from crossing multiple time zones produces acute **circadian misalignment** that can degrade athletic performance.  When athletes travel internationally, their internal clocks become transiently desynchronized from local time, disrupting sleep–wake cycles and physiology.  In practice, eastward travel (advancing the clock) often causes more pronounced decrements than westward shifts, as reported in recent work where East-West jet lag significantly reduced home teams’ win probabilities and scoring efficiency.  These observations align with our data showing declines in coordination, reaction time, and overall output following long-haul flights.  In summary, the consensus is that travel fatigue and jet lag pose a *real risk* to performance, and our results support this by demonstrating worse outcomes with greater time-zone shifts.

Long-haul travel also **impairs sleep** in athletes, which likely mediates performance loss.  Objective monitoring (actigraphy) and sleep diaries reveal that long flights (>3 time zones) markedly reduce sleep duration, time-in-bed, and sleep efficiency for 1–2 days after arrival.  In our study the first 48 h post-travel saw significantly shorter and more fragmented sleep, along with increased daytime fatigue, consistent with these reports.  Such sleep deficits can blunt training adaptations and alertness.  For example, large-scale sensor studies link shorter sleep and later sleep timing to reduced next-day physical activity.  Similarly, intense evening exercise (or competition) may further delay sleep onset, showing a dose–response relationship where later or more evening training worsens sleep metrics.  These findings explain how jet lag’s sleep disruption plausibly underpins the performance declines we observed.

Empirical evidence supports a **jet lag–performance link** in competition.  In basketball, for instance, multiyear analyses showed that advanced (eastward) circadian shifts significantly lower key game metrics: home teams returned from eastward trips scored fewer points, secured fewer rebounds, and had worse shooting percentages compared to neutral travel.  Likewise, retrospective NBA data (2014–2018) indicate that both circadian displacement and longer flight distances correlate with poorer game performance (lower point differentials), even after adjusting for team strength.  Our results mirror these patterns: games played soon after long eastward flights had notably worse outcomes.  We assessed these effects using mixed linear models (treating team and opponent as random factors), following recommended sport-science methods.  These statistical approaches improve sensitivity to travel effects by exploiting repeated measures.

For statistical rigor, we employed **mixed-effects modeling** to analyze our longitudinal performance data.  This approach accounts for repeated measures within athletes and games.  Consistent with best practices in exercise physiology, we treated travel direction and distance as fixed effects and teams/matches as random effects.  This framework, advocated in recent sports studies, helps isolate the influence of circadian variables on outcomes.  Finally, expert consensus stresses that, given limited direct evidence, practitioners should still proactively manage jet lag with chronobiology-based strategies.  For example, scheduling pre-travel light exposure and gradual phase shifts is recommended to mitigate the impairments we and others have documented.
